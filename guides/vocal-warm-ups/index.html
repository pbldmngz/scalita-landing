Vocal warm-ups

Three routines from the Scalita app, played on a real piano in your range: a quick warm-up for right before you sing, a full warm-up, and a cool-down for after. Choose the voice type closest to yours, or set your own lowest and highest notes, and press play.

Each routine here is the one in the app, step for step: the same patterns, syllables, tempos and order. What a video can't do is change key for you. Here the piano moves every pattern a half step at a time to fit the range you set, so you sing in your own voice instead of the voice of whoever recorded it.

Not sure which one fits today? Answer the two Find My Warmup questions on this page, the same ones the app asks. If you don't know your range yet, the vocal range test finds your lowest and highest notes.

Which one today?

Find My Warmup asks how your voice feels before it asks what you want to work on, and the first answer can overrule the second.

  • Tired / strained always leads to the cool-down, played a little slower, whatever you wanted to work on.
  • Quick pre-show leads to the quick warm-up, and Just warm me up to the full one.
  • A bit off slows any routine down a little, and the app holds back Extend my range and Grit / screaming for the day.
  • Ready to go plays the routines a little faster.

How these warm-ups are built

All three start with a trill on a five-note scale. Trills and hums partly close the mouth. Singing teachers and voice therapists have used them for a long time, and many warm-ups, these included, begin with them. In a survey of 117 singers, five-note scales and arpeggios were among the exercises used most.

From there the patterns widen: five notes, then an arpeggio across an octave, then, in the two warm-ups, an octave and a half. Each exercise starts on a comfortable key rather than your lowest, climbs to the top of its range, then comes back down through the keys to the bottom.

Some steps are placed around your voice break instead. Approach only stops just under the break, and Cross once goes over it and back. Between keys, the piano plays a short chord to give you the new key and a beat to breathe.

Common questions

How long should a vocal warm-up be?

There's no agreed number. In a survey of 117 singers, the warm-up length they preferred most was 5 to 10 minutes. On a two-octave range, the routines here take 6 to 8 minutes (quick), 12 to 14 (full) and 7 to 11 (cool-down).

Are there different warm-ups for female and male voices?

The exercises are the same for everyone. What changes is the key: pick the preset closest to your voice, from soprano down to bass, or set your own range, and the piano moves every pattern to fit. A preset only sets where the piano plays, so choose it by where your voice sits rather than by gender. The voice types page describes each one.

Do vocal warm-ups actually work?

Singers feel them. In one study, all twelve classically trained singers noticed changes in their tone and control after 25 minutes of warming up. Listeners heard a difference less often, mostly in singers whose vibrato had evened out. A warm-up is mainly for you: it's how you find today's voice before the singing counts.

Should I warm up when I am sick?

No. If your throat hurts or your voice is hoarse, rest it, and see a doctor if it doesn't clear up. The cool-down is for a voice that's tired from singing, not for one that's ill.
